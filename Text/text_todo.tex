## 1. The core argument

The paper is a computational-methods paper with an economic-interpretation selling point. Its chain of reasoning is:

EGM is fast because, with concave value functions, the Euler equation is necessary *and* sufficient. In discrete-continuous (DC) models the discrete choice puts a kink in the value function and a jump in the marginal value function, so the Euler equation has multiple roots and the EGM step produces candidate points that are not optimal. Worse, each period's kinks are inherited by earlier periods through backward induction. The existing fix (Fella, Druedahl–Jørgensen, DC-EGM, FUES) is to generate the suboptimal points and then detect and discard them, which costs an extra step per period per state.

Iskhakov et al. (2017) offer an alternative: add Extreme Value Type I taste shocks, which replace the hard max with a log-sum and thereby "concavify" the problem. The paper's complaint is not that this fails but that it is *unmotivated* — the shocks are behavioral randomization bolted on for numerical convenience.

The paper's proposal is to get the same smoothing from an *endogenous* margin: the agent chooses search intensity $\varepsilon_t \in [0,1]$ at convex cost $\sigma(\varepsilon)=S[\varepsilon+(1-\varepsilon)\log(1-\varepsilon)]$, and $\varepsilon_t$ becomes the weight on the "switch" branch. Because $\varepsilon^*$ responds continuously to the value gap between branches, the combined value function is smooth, the EGM step stops producing suboptimal points, and the envelope step can be dropped. The claimed advantages over taste shocks are all interpretive rather than numerical: search is a real economic friction, its magnitude is disciplined by observable search durations, it only moves agents who are near the switching threshold (so the Euler equation retains its meaning elsewhere), and because the cost is homogeneous it can be normalized by permanent income without adding a state variable.

This is demonstrated in two applications. In the retirement model, effort at $t$ raises the probability of receiving a retirement *option* at $t+1$, and a mean-one lognormal multiplicative taste shock $\xi$ scales the worker's value. In the durable-goods (S,s) model, $\varepsilon_t$ is the contemporaneous probability of successfully adjusting the durable stock, with EV Type I taste shocks and stochastic durable prices offered as alternative smoothers. A separate subsection argues that coarse income discretization creates "phantom kinks" — numerical wiggles that must not be mistaken for economic discontinuities — and that a transition-matrix integration of the continuous income density removes them.

## 2. The logical structure, and where it holds

Written as a chain, the argument is:

**(P1)** Hard discrete choice ⇒ $v_m$ discontinuous. **(P2)** $v_m$ discontinuous ⇒ EGM's endogenous grid is non-monotone ⇒ suboptimal points. **(P3)** Search makes the combined value function smooth. **(P4)** Smooth ⇒ no suboptimal points ⇒ no envelope step needed ⇒ cheaper. **(P5)** Search is preferable to taste shocks because it is microfounded, testable, locally targeted, and normalizable.

P1 and P2 are standard and correctly stated. P5 is partly sound and partly overstated. **P3 and P4 are where the argument has real gaps**, and one of them is fixable in a way that would make the paper considerably stronger.

### The smoothing claim is provable, and proving it resolves an apparent self-contradiction

The paper establishes P3 visually — figures of consumption rules and S,s regions that look smoother. But the search-smoothed value function has a closed form. Substituting $\varepsilon^*=1-e^{-\Delta/S}$ with $\Delta \equiv v^{\text{adj}}-v^{\text{keep}}$ back into the objective gives

$$v_t = v^{\text{keep}} + \varphi(\Delta), \qquad \varphi(\Delta)=\begin{cases}\Delta - S\left(1-e^{-\Delta/S}\right) & \Delta>0\\ 0 & \Delta\le 0\end{cases}$$

so $\varphi(0)=0$, $\varphi'(0^\pm)=0$, but $\varphi''(0^-)=0$ while $\varphi''(0^+)=1/S$. **Search-smoothing is exactly $C^1$ and not $C^2$, with a second-derivative jump of $1/S$ at the threshold.** The EV Type I log-sum, by contrast, gives $v_t = v^{\text{keep}} + \sigma_\varepsilon\log(1+e^{\Delta/\sigma_\varepsilon})$, which is real-analytic everywhere.

This has three consequences that the paper should be making explicitly rather than leaving to the reader's eye:

First, it *vindicates* the core computational claim on firmer ground than figures. What kills suboptimal points in the EGM step is **continuity of $v_m$**, and search delivers that ($v_m=(1-\varepsilon^*)\lambda^{\text{keep}}+\varepsilon^*\lambda^{\text{adj}}$ is continuous because $\varepsilon^*\to 0$ continuously). What kills MPC spikes is **differentiability of $v_m$**, and search does not deliver that.

Second, it explains the paper's own Figure 2(b), where smoothing appears only to the right of the discontinuity. The paper attributes this to an economic story (poor workers don't search). The deeper reason is structural: the $\max\{0,\cdot\}$ in the effort rule makes the smoothing kernel strictly one-sided, and the curvature is $0$ on one side and $1/S$ on the other. That asymmetry is a property of the functional form, not of the calibration, so no choice of $S$ fixes it.

Third — and this is the payoff — it dissolves what currently reads as a contradiction between the abstract and the conclusion. The abstract says "with ample search frictions, the suboptimal points can be eliminated entirely"; the conclusion says "to get uniform smoothness around the discontinuities, we need to incorporate taste shocks as well." Those are reconcilable only if eliminating suboptimal points and achieving uniform smoothness are different requirements — which is precisely the $C^1$-versus-$C^2$ distinction. The paper is implicitly relying on that distinction without ever stating it, so as written the two claims look inconsistent.

### The efficiency claim (P4) currently has no supporting evidence, and the code contradicts it

The abstract and conclusion both claim search eliminates "an additional, computationally costly, step." Three problems:

The code does not do this. `DurableModel.py` still runs the HARK `upper_envelope` on the keeper (around line 1007) and `FUES_allPol` on the adjuster with search switched on, and `Endogenous-Retirement-Search-v3.py` still calls `calcMultilineEnvelope` in `_finish_solveWorkingDeaton`. So the central cost saving is asserted but never taken.

There is also a reason it *cannot* be taken in the durable model. Search smooths the *combination* of the keep and adjust branches. The adjuster's own first-order condition in $d$ has multiple roots because the adjustment cost is non-convex — a source of suboptimal points that is logically independent of the discrete keep/adjust margin and that no amount of $\varepsilon$-smoothing touches. Section 3 concedes exactly this ("even with this algorithm, small discontinuities still arise in the adjuster policy functions"), and then the conclusion claims the envelope step becomes unnecessary. That is an internal contradiction, and it is the one I would fix first.

Finally, there is no timing evidence anywhere for search versus DC-EGM/FUES. The only solve-time table in the paper is Table 1, which is about income discretization.

### There is no accuracy benchmark at all

The code contains sanity assertions (assets non-negative, adjustment probabilities interior when $S>0$), budget residual checks, and solve timings, but no Euler-equation errors, no value-function-iteration or brute-force benchmark, and no convergence test of the search solution against a very fine grid of the *same* model. For a paper whose thesis is "the solution is now smooth," this is the central missing evidence, because a smooth wrong answer is strictly worse than a jagged right one. Without residual diagnostics, the figures cannot distinguish "search removed the suboptimal points" from "search averaged over them."

Relatedly: `directed_search_function_scaled_matrix` in `utility.py` picks $\varepsilon$ by `argmax` over a 101-point grid rather than using the closed form the appendix derives. So any residual roughness in the reported policies could be an $\varepsilon$-grid artifact — exactly the kind of numerical-versus-economic confound the phantom-kinks section warns against.

## 3. Paradoxes and unresolved tensions

**Solving a model versus changing it.** The framing is "new methods for efficiently solving" DC models, but search frictions change the model. The paper's own results confirm this: the consumption function shifts up with $S$ and down with $\sigma_\xi$. So this is not error reduction, it is a different economy solved exactly. The paper's defense — search "rationalizes the smoothness into the agent's choice problem" — is the right defense but is asserted rather than developed. And there is a trade-off it never states: fidelity to the frictionless target model requires $S\to 0$, which is precisely the limit where $\varepsilon^*\to\mathbb{1}\{\cdot\}$ and the discontinuities return. Smoothness and fidelity are in direct conflict, and the paper never quantifies the exchange rate.

**The abstract's headline is achieved trivially in the limit.** "With ample search frictions the suboptimal points can be eliminated entirely" is true, but Section 3 also notes that as $S\to\infty$, $\varepsilon^*\to 0$ and the household never adjusts. In that limit the discrete choice is smoothed away by being removed. So the substantive claim has to be "at empirically plausible $S$ you get enough smoothness" — which makes the calibration argument load-bearing rather than a nice-to-have.

**The rationalization advantage may not survive.** For two alternatives, a search technology with a convex control cost is formally a perturbed-utility (additive-cost) discrete choice, and additive-random-utility and additive-perturbed-utility discrete choice are observationally equivalent classes (Hofbauer–Sandholm; Fudenberg–Strzalecki). The paper's cost function is not Bernoulli entropy — it lacks the $\varepsilon\log\varepsilon$ term — which is exactly why it yields the one-sided $\varepsilon^*=\max\{0,1-e^{-\Delta/S}\}$ rather than the two-sided logit. So the difference between "search" and "taste shocks" is the *shape of the smoothing kernel*, not rational-versus-behavioral. This does not sink the paper, but it means the "rationalizes" framing should be demoted and the two claims that genuinely survive — one-sidedness and testability — promoted.

**The paper wants one-sidedness and two-sidedness simultaneously.** This is the sharpest tension. The economic case against taste shocks (Introduction, citing McKay–Wieland's finding that three quarters of adjusters adjust because of match-quality shocks) is that they put probability mass on the dominated branch far from the threshold. Search avoids this by construction, since $\varepsilon^*=0$ whenever $\Delta\le 0$. But that same one-sidedness is why search alone cannot deliver uniform smoothness, so the paper adds taste shocks — reintroducing exactly the dominated-branch adjusters it objected to. The argument is only won if the reintroduced share is small, and that number is computable from the existing code but is nowhere reported. **This is the single most valuable missing piece of evidence in the paper.**

**The two applications implement different mechanisms and reach opposite verdicts on combining them.** In the retirement model, effort at $t$ shifts the arrival of an *option* at $t+1$ (the FOC carries $\beta(1-p)$), a genuine dynamic search technology. In the durable model, $\varepsilon_t$ is a contemporaneous mixing weight with no lag, no baseline arrival rate $p$, and no state that records having searched. They are presented as the same device. Section 3 then says nesting search with taste shocks "would double-smooth the discrete choice without a clear economic interpretation," while Section 2 *requires* that combination for uniform smoothness. Both cannot be right.

**The multiplicative taste shock is not invariant to a normalization the code chooses arbitrarily.** In the retirement model $\xi$ multiplies $V^W$. But a value function is only pinned down up to an affine transformation of flow utility, and `Endogenous-Retirement-Search-v3.py` line 511 hardcodes exactly such a shift:

```509:511:c:\Users\amonninger\OneDrive - Texas A&M University\GitHub\AMonninger\DCEGM_Search\Code\Endogenous-Retirement-Search-v3.py
        # d == 2 is working
        # - 10.0 moves curve down to improve linear interpolation
```

With log utility $V^W<0$, so $\xi>1$ makes working *worse*, and the effective size of the shock scales with the level of $V^W$ — which the $-10.0$ sets. This also cuts against the Introduction's own argument #3, which criticizes taste shocks for being "the same for each household independent of income or wealth": in the paper's own retirement implementation the shock's effect scales with the value level and therefore with wealth. The move to additive EV shocks in the durable model looks like a fix for this, but the switch is never disclosed as such — and the Introduction still describes the paper's taste shocks as log-normal, while `DurableModel.py` raises a `ValueError` if `sigma_xi` is passed.

**The search cost is a utility cost, but the microfoundation offered is a resource cost.** Introduction #3 justifies normalization by arguing "the cost of searching is time spent searching which is related to the personal wage rate." A time cost belongs in the budget constraint (or as forgone income), which would change the Euler equation and the EGM inversion. In the model and code it is subtracted from utility. The justification and the implementation do not match, and the utility-cost version faces the same "in what units is $S$?" question the paper poses to $\sigma_\varepsilon$.

**Cross-mechanism rankings use incommensurable units.** The Introduction ranks search as providing "the highest degree of smoothness," while Section 3 says search achieves "similar smoothing compared to the EV shocks." Neither can be evaluated, because $S$, $\sigma_\varepsilon$, $\sigma_\xi$, $p$, and $\sigma_\eta$ have no common scale. Any ranking needs a normalization — equalize the implied adjustment hazard, or the share of non-Euler adjusters, or the welfare cost of the friction — and then compare residual non-smoothness.

**The testability argument overreaches slightly.** "If households optimally have an intensity of 0.5, we would assume it takes them on average two periods to find a car" requires a constant hazard over the spell, but $\varepsilon^*$ depends on $(m_t,n_t,p_t)$, which evolve, and the durable model has no search-spell state — an unsuccessful searcher is indistinguishable from a non-searcher. Also, duration data identifies $S$ only jointly with $\Delta$, which is model-generated, so it is not "directly measurable." The defensible version is stronger than it sounds: duration data supplies an over-identifying moment that a taste-shock model has *no* implication for at all.

## 4. The phantom-kinks section

This is the cleanest-reasoned part of the paper, and it is unusually candid about its own limits, but it has two problems and one place where the repo contradicts the text.

The experiment does not isolate what it claims to. It sets $p=1$, $\sigma_s=0$, $\sigma_\xi=0$ — the configuration in which the *economic* kinks are largest (Figure 2a). So the plotted curve contains both artifact and signal, and the paper's own "Benchmarking caveat" concedes that the natural quantitative test does not discriminate. A clean test is available and cheap: solve the pure consumption-saving model with no retirement option, where the true policy is provably smooth and monotone, and any non-monotonicity at coarse $N$ is unambiguously numerical.

The $N=1$ case is mislabeled. One equiprobable node of a mean-one lognormal is degenerate at 1, so $N=1$ is the *deterministic-income* model, not a coarse approximation of the $\sigma_\eta=\sqrt{0.005}$ model. Its sawtooth is the true kink structure of a different model, not a discretization artifact. Note also that max deviation is essentially identical for $N=1$ and $N=7$ (4.72 versus 4.68) while mean deviation differs almost tenfold — the max is being driven by threshold placement near a discontinuity, which is exactly why a max-abs metric over a region containing kinks is uninformative here.

Table 1 undercuts its own conclusion. The text says $N=31$ already damps the artifacts, and $N=31$ takes 1.60s against the transition matrix's 4.10s. So the honest comparison makes the matrix about 2.6× *slower* for the same qualitative smoothness; the cost advantage exists only against $N=151$, which the text itself says is more than needed. And `Protocol.txt` in the repo records the authors' own finding: "Transitionmatrix: not more efficient than 151 version..." That is a direct evidence-versus-claim conflict inside the repository.

## 5. What would strengthen the paper most

Ranked by how much they move the argument:

State and prove the smoothness result. Replace "the figures look smooth" with a proposition: search-smoothing yields $v_t\in C^1$ with $\varphi''$ jumping by $1/S$ at the threshold, hence $v_m$ continuous (no suboptimal points) but kinked (MPC jumps); log-sum yields $C^\infty$. This upgrades the central claim, explains Figure 2(b)'s asymmetry structurally, and removes the apparent abstract-versus-conclusion inconsistency.

Report the share of adjusters who are taste-driven at the $(S,\sigma_\xi)$ pair that achieves smoothness. This is the decisive test of the paper's economic selling point against McKay–Wieland, it is computable from the existing code, and it is currently absent.

Add residual diagnostics. Max and mean Euler error, and a count of non-monotonicities in the raw EGM cloud, both as functions of $S$. This converts "with ample search frictions the suboptimal points can be eliminated entirely" from an eyeballed claim into a measured one, and identifies the threshold $S$ at which the envelope step can genuinely be dropped.

Actually drop the envelope step and time it, or explain precisely why it must be retained for the adjuster's non-convexity. Right now the paper's headline cost saving is claimed while the code still pays it.

Reconcile the two applications. Either make the durable $\varepsilon_t$ a genuine one-period-ahead option-arrival technology like the retirement version, or state clearly that they are different devices sharing a cost function, and resolve the contradiction over whether search and taste shocks should be combined.

Put the mechanisms on a common scale before ranking them, and drop the "rationalized versus behavioral" framing in favour of one-sidedness plus testability, which are the two claims that survive the observational-equivalence objection.

Fix the level-dependence of the multiplicative retirement taste shock, or switch it to additive as in the durable model and say so. The hardcoded $-10.0$ currently makes results depend on an arbitrary normalization.

Redo the phantom-kinks experiment on a model with a provably smooth solution, and either restate the cost claim against $N=31$ or explain why $N=151$ is the right comparison.

Two smaller items: the notebooks use $\alpha=0.75$, $\tau=0.3$, $\delta=0.05$, $\beta=0.975$ while `DurableModel.py` defaults to $\alpha=0.9$, $\tau=0.15$, $\delta=0.15$, $\beta=0.96$, so the figures are not reproducible from the module defaults, and $\tau=0.3$ is a large adjustment cost that maximizes the non-convexity — a sensitivity check nearer Berger–Vavra values would help. And the durable figures use a five-period horizon plotted one period from the terminal, which is thin support for a claim about kinks *accumulating* through backward iteration; the retirement model's $T=20$ at $t=15$ is much better suited to that claim.
