\section{Durable model (Basic model)}\label{sec:DurableModelBasic}

The second example is a consumption-savings model with durable and nondurable goods. It is a simplified version of the model in \cite{Monninger2025-cz} which uses endogeneous search as a numerical tool to solve the model. This section focuses on the solution method and compares the usage of taste shocks, endogeneous search, and stochastic durable prices.

\subsection{Model setup}\label{sec:ModelBasic}
The model consists of ex-ante identical agents who face uninsurable income risk and can consume non-durable goods, receive flow utility from a durable good, and can save in one single risk-free liquit asset.

\begin{figure}[h!]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{timeline_DurableModel.pdf}
	\caption{Timeline of the durable model}
	\label{fig:timeline_DurableModel}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline_DurableModel} places search effort before the price draw. The household starts with durable stock $n_t$ and market resources $m_t$, and chooses effort from the distribution of the purchase price. A match draws that price, and the household accepts the draw or keeps the current stock. The keeper then chooses nondurable consumption and assets. The adjuster chooses nondurable consumption, a new stock, and assets at the realized price. The period ends with $(d_t,a_t)$. Between $t$ and $t+1$, interest is received, the transitory income shock realizes, and the durable depreciates. With search and price dispersion shut off, this order collapses to a hard maximum over the keeper and the adjuster. That baseline follows. Search and the price draw return together in Section~\ref{subsec:ModelBasicSearch}.

Each household is characterized by two state variables: beginning-of-period market resources $m_t$ and the stock of durables $n_t$. Preferences exhibit constant relative risk aversion and combine nondurable consumption $c_t$ and the service flow of durables $d_t$ using a Cobb-Douglas aggregator\footnote{Using this form is standard (eg see \cite{Berger2015-kp, McKay2021-ib}) which is in line with micro-evidence \cite{Berger2018-yb}. An alternative approach is to use a CES aggregator as in \cite{Mankiw1983-za}}:
\begin{align}
	u(c_t, d_t) &= \frac{\big(c_t^\alpha d_t^{1-\alpha}\big)^{1-\rho}}{1-\rho}, \qquad \alpha \in (0,1), \ \rho > 0.
\end{align}

At the start of the period, the household observes $(m_t, n_t)$ and decides whether to keep the durable stock unchanged or to adjust. If no adjustment occurs, the household sets $d_t = n_t$ and allocates $m_t$ between nondurable consumption and savings. If the household adjusts, it sells the entire beginning-of-period stock at no price risk but incurs a proportional adjustment cost $\tau \in (0,1)$. The resulting liquidity is
\begin{align}
	\ell_t = m_t + (1-\tau)n_t,
\end{align}
which can then be allocated across nondurable consumption, a new durable stock $d_t$, and savings $a_t$.

The household's problem is the hard discrete choice between keeping and adjusting:
\begin{align}
	v_t(m_t,n_t) = \max\Big\{ v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t),\; v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t)\Big\},
\end{align}
where $v_t^{\text{keep}}$ and $v_t^{\text{adj}}$ are the value functions conditional on keeping or adjusting.

Labor income is idiosyncratic and purely transitory. Normalized income is
\begin{align}
	y_t = \theta_t,
\end{align}
where $\theta_t$ is i.i.d.\ log-normally distributed with $\mathbb{E}[\theta_t] = 1$ and a strictly positive standard deviation. There is no unemployment risk.

Assets earn a single interest rate $r$, so the gross interest factor is $\mathcal{R} = 1+r$. The durable stock depreciates at rate $\delta \in (0,1)$. There are no maintenance costs and no operation costs. Therefore, next-period states evolve according to
\begin{align}
	m_{t+1} &= \mathcal{R} a_t + \theta_{t+1},\\
	n_{t+1} &= (1-\delta)\, d_t.
\end{align}

Borrowing is prohibited: the loan-to-value ratio is zero and the lower bound on assets is $\underline{a}=0$, so end-of-period assets must satisfy $a_t \ge 0$.

Hence, the Bellman equations conditional on keep and adjust are:
\begin{align}
	v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t) &= \max_{c_t} \ u(c_t ,d_t) + \beta \mathbb{E}_t \big[ v_{t+1}(m_{t+1},n_{t+1}) \big],\\
	& \text{s.t. } a_t = m_t - c_t \geq 0,\\
	& \qquad d_t = n_t,\\
	& \qquad m_{t+1} = \mathcal{R} a_t + \theta_{t+1},\\
	& \qquad n_{t+1} = (1-\delta)\, d_t.\\[1em]
	v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t) &= \max_{c_t,d_t} \ u(c_t,d_t) + \beta \mathbb{E}_t \big[ v_{t+1}(m_{t+1},n_{t+1}) \big],\\
	& \text{s.t. } a_t = \ell_t - c_t - d_t \geq 0,\\
	& \qquad m_{t+1} = \mathcal{R} a_t + \theta_{t+1},\\
	& \qquad n_{t+1} = (1-\delta)\, d_t.
\end{align}

With the following first-order conditions with respect to nondurable consumption and durable stock:
\begin{align}
	\frac{\partial v_t^{adj}}{\partial c_t} &= u_c(c_t, d_t) \geq \beta \mathcal{R}\, \mathbb{E}_t \big[v_{t+1}^m\big], \label{eq:FOC_c_basic} \\
	\frac{\partial v_t^{adj}}{\partial d_t} &= u_d(c_t, d_t) \geq \beta \mathcal{R}\, \mathbb{E}_t\big[v_{t+1}^m\big] - \beta (1 - \delta)\, \mathbb{E}_t\big[v_{t+1}^n\big], \label{eq:FOC_d_basic}
\end{align}

Equations~\ref{eq:FOC_c_basic} and~\ref{eq:FOC_d_basic} are stated as KKT conditions. Equality holds when the borrowing constraint does not bind, and the left-hand side strictly exceeds the right-hand side when it does. Equation~\ref{eq:FOC_c_basic} equates the marginal utility of nondurable consumption with the discounted shadow value of savings. Equation~\ref{eq:FOC_d_basic} shows that the marginal utility of durables equals the net user cost: the first term on the right is the discounted opportunity cost $\mathcal{R}$ of diverting a unit of resources into durables rather than liquid assets, and the second term is the discounted benefit of carrying the remaining durable stock $(1-\delta)d_t$ into the next period.

\subsection{Algorithm}\label{subsec:AlgorithmBasic}
This section outlines the algorithm for one recursive period of the baseline model: no Extreme Value Type~I taste shocks, no directed search, and no durable-price dispersion. The discrete keep-versus-adjust choice is resolved by a hard maximum. The terminal period is closed form---the keeper sets $c_T=m_T$ and $d_T=n_T$, the adjuster sets $c_T=\alpha\ell_T$ and $d_T=(1-\alpha)\ell_T$, and the two branches are combined by a hard max---so Algorithm~\ref{alg:durable_baseline} starts from a given next-period solution $(v_{t+1},\,v_{t+1}^{m},\,v_{t+1}^{n})$.

Define the end-of-period (gothic) objects on $(d_t,a_t)$ by
\begin{align}
	\mathfrak{v}_c'(d_t,a_t) &= \beta\,\mathcal{R}\,\mathbb{E}_t\big[v_{t+1}^{m}(n_{t+1},m_{t+1})\big],\\
	\mathfrak{v}_d'(d_t,a_t) &= \beta\,(1-\delta)\,\mathbb{E}_t\big[v_{t+1}^{n}(n_{t+1},m_{t+1})\big],\\
	\mathfrak{v}(d_t,a_t) &= \beta\,\mathbb{E}_t\big[v_{t+1}(n_{t+1},m_{t+1})\big],
\end{align}
with $m_{t+1}=\mathcal{R}a_t+\theta_{t+1}$ and $n_{t+1}=(1-\delta)d_t$. The expectation over $\theta_{t+1}$ may be evaluated by quadrature (as written below) or by the transition-matrix integration described in Appendix~\ref{sec:appdx_model_basic}. Because adjustment costs make the first-order conditions only necessary, the keeper step applies a DC-EGM upper envelope \citep{Iskhakov2017-jb} and the adjuster step applies FUES \citep{Dobrescu2022-ii}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{EGM step for the baseline durable model (hard max)}
\label{alg:durable_baseline}
\DontPrintSemicolon
Let $\vec{\theta}=\{\theta_1,\dots,\theta_J\}$ be quadrature points for the transitory income shock with weights $\vec{\omega}=\{\omega_1,\dots,\omega_J\}$.\;
Form ascending grids $\vec{a}_t=\{a^1,\dots,a^I\}$, $\vec{d}_t=\{d^1,\dots,d^L\}$, a fine durable grid $\vec{d}_t^{\mathrm{adj}}$ for the adjuster FOC scan, and beginning-of-period grids $\vec{n}_t$, $\vec{m}_t$.\;
\BlankLine
\tcc{End-of-period (gothic) functions}
\For{$i = 1,\dots,I$}{
	\For{$q = 1,\dots,L$}{
		\For{$j = 1,\dots,J$}{
			Compute $m_{t+1}^{j}(a^i)=\mathcal{R}a^i+\theta_j$ and $n_{t+1}(d^q)=(1-\delta)d^q$\;
			Interpolate $v_{t+1}$, $v_{t+1}^{m}$, and $v_{t+1}^{n}$ at $(n_{t+1}(d^q),\,m_{t+1}^{j}(a^i))$\;
		}
		Compute $\mathfrak{v}_c'(d^q,a^i)=\beta\mathcal{R}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\omega_j\,v_{t+1}^{m}(n_{t+1}(d^q),m_{t+1}^{j}(a^i))$\;
		Compute $\mathfrak{v}_d'(d^q,a^i)=\beta(1-\delta)\sum_{j=1}^{J}\omega_j\,v_{t+1}^{n}(n_{t+1}(d^q),m_{t+1}^{j}(a^i))$\;
		Compute $\mathfrak{v}(d^q,a^i)=\beta\sum_{j=1}^{J}\omega_j\,v_{t+1}(n_{t+1}(d^q),m_{t+1}^{j}(a^i))$\;
	}
}
\BlankLine
\tcc{Keeper: EGM with $d_t=n_t$, then DC-EGM upper envelope}
\For{each $n\in\vec{n}_t$}{
	\For{$i = 1,\dots,I$}{
		Compute $c_t^{\mathrm{keep}}(a^i;n)=u_c^{-1}\big(\mathfrak{v}_c'(n,a^i);\,n\big)$\;
		Compute endogenous resources $m_t^{\mathrm{EGM}}(a^i;n)=a^i+c_t^{\mathrm{keep}}(a^i;n)$\;
		Compute $v_t^{\mathrm{EGM}}(a^i;n)=u\big(c_t^{\mathrm{keep}}(a^i;n),n\big)+\mathfrak{v}(n,a^i)$\;
	}
	Apply a DC-EGM upper envelope to $\{(m_t^{\mathrm{EGM}},\,c_t^{\mathrm{keep}},\,v_t^{\mathrm{EGM}})\}$ and interpolate onto $\vec{m}_t$ to obtain $c_t^{\mathrm{keep}}(n,m)$, $a_t^{\mathrm{keep}}=\max\{m-c_t^{\mathrm{keep}},0\}$, and $d_t^{\mathrm{keep}}=n$\;
	Compute $v_t^{\mathrm{keep}}(n,m)=u(c_t^{\mathrm{keep}},n)+\mathfrak{v}(n,a_t^{\mathrm{keep}})$, $v_t^{m,\mathrm{keep}}=u_c(c_t^{\mathrm{keep}},n)$, and $v_t^{n,\mathrm{keep}}=u_d(c_t^{\mathrm{keep}},n)+\mathfrak{v}_d'(n,a_t^{\mathrm{keep}})$\;
}
\BlankLine
\tcc{Adjuster: FOC roots in $a$ on a fine $d$-grid, then FUES in liquidity}
Initialize empty candidate clouds for $(\ell_t,c_t,d_t,a_t,v_t)$\;
\For{each $d\in\vec{d}_t^{\mathrm{adj}}$}{
	Find all roots $a$ of $u_d(c,d)-\mathfrak{v}_c'(d,a)+\mathfrak{v}_d'(d,a)=0$ with $c=u_c^{-1}\big(\mathfrak{v}_c'(d,a);\,d\big)$\;
	\For{each root $a$}{
		Compute $\ell_t=a+c+d$ and $v_t^{\mathrm{adj}}=u(c,d)+\mathfrak{v}(d,a)$\;
		Append $(\ell_t,c,d,a,v_t^{\mathrm{adj}})$ to the candidate cloud\;
	}
}
Sort candidates by $\ell_t$ and apply FUES to obtain refined points $(\ell_t,c_t^{\mathrm{adj}},d_t^{\mathrm{adj}},a_t^{\mathrm{adj}},v_t^{\mathrm{adj}})$\;
Construct one-dimensional interpolants $c_t^{\mathrm{adj}}(\ell)$, $d_t^{\mathrm{adj}}(\ell)$, and $a_t^{\mathrm{adj}}(\ell)$\;
\For{each $(n,m)\in\vec{n}_t\times\vec{m}_t$}{
	Compute $\ell_t=m+(1-\tau)n$\;
	Evaluate $c_t^{\mathrm{adj}}(\ell_t)$, $d_t^{\mathrm{adj}}(\ell_t)$, and $a_t^{\mathrm{adj}}=\max\{\ell_t-c_t^{\mathrm{adj}}-d_t^{\mathrm{adj}},0\}$\;
	Compute $v_t^{\mathrm{adj}}(\ell_t)=u(c_t^{\mathrm{adj}},d_t^{\mathrm{adj}})+\mathfrak{v}(d_t^{\mathrm{adj}},a_t^{\mathrm{adj}})$, $v_t^{m,\mathrm{adj}}=u_c(c_t^{\mathrm{adj}},d_t^{\mathrm{adj}})$, and $v_t^{n,\mathrm{adj}}=(1-\tau)\,u_c(c_t^{\mathrm{adj}},d_t^{\mathrm{adj}})$\;
}
\BlankLine
\tcc{Hard-max discrete choice}
\For{each $(n,m)\in\vec{n}_t\times\vec{m}_t$}{
	Set $\mathcal{I}_t(n,m)=1$ if $v_t^{\mathrm{adj}}\big(m+(1-\tau)n\big)\ge v_t^{\mathrm{keep}}(n,m)$ and $0$ otherwise\;
	Set $(c_t,d_t,a_t,v_t,v_t^{m},v_t^{n})$ to the adjuster branch if $\mathcal{I}_t=1$ and to the keeper branch if $\mathcal{I}_t=0$\;
}
Collect the combined policies and (inverse) value functions on $(\vec{n}_t,\vec{m}_t)$ for use in period $t-1$.\;
\end{algorithm}

The upper left panel is the adjustment region in $(m_t,n_t)$. The household adjusts with probability one in one region and keeps with probability one in the other. The boundary between them is the $(S,s)$ band. Inside it, the beginning-of-period stock is close enough to the stock the household would choose that the proportional cost is not worth paying. Outside it, the household pays the cost and reoptimizes. Because the choice is an indicator, the combined durable policy in the upper right panel jumps between the keeper, who is stuck at $d_T = n_T$, and the adjuster, who sets $d_T = (1-\alpha)\ell_T$. The lower panels hold the beginning-of-period stock fixed and plot the combined rule together with the two branches it switches between. Where the band is crossed, the marginal propensity inherits the jump. The spike is the discrete change of branch, rather than a feature of either branch's own Euler equation.

In every earlier period the household anticipates that jump. Next period's value is kinked along the future band, so today's first-order conditions are necessary and not sufficient, and the endogenous grid for the keeper can fold back on itself. The adjuster's scan of the durable first-order condition likewise returns several candidates. Algorithm~\ref{alg:durable_baseline} therefore applies an upper envelope on each branch, DC-EGM for the keeper and FUES for the adjuster. The envelope removes the dominated candidates on a branch. Joining the two refined branches by a hard maximum puts the jump back into the combined policy, and that combined policy is the continuation of the previous period. The nonconvexity is passed backward, as in the retirement problem. Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_t_MPC_decomp} shows the resulting rules at a fixed beginning-of-period stock. Where adjusting is optimal, the combined rule and its marginal propensity lie on the adjuster. Once market resources cross the band they lie on the keeper, and both marginal propensities spike at the crossing. The extensions that follow replace this indicator with a probability. The remainder of the section asks whether that replacement removes the spike, and whether the three replacements can be told apart.

\subsection{Extension: Extreme Value Type I Taste Shocks}\label{subsec:ModelBasicTaste}

The hard maximum in the discrete keep-versus-adjust decision produces a kink in the value function and a discontinuity in the policy functions at the state combinations $(m_t,n_t)$ where the household is indifferent between the two branches. Following the discrete--continuous choice literature (e.g.\ \cite{Iskhakov2017-jb}), this subsection smooths that discontinuity with i.i.d.\ Extreme Value Type I (Gumbel) taste shocks on the two discrete alternatives, while leaving search frictions aside (these are introduced separately in Section~\ref{subsec:ModelBasicSearch}). EV Type I shocks enter \emph{additively} on each branch and are integrated out in closed form: they randomize the discrete outcome directly through logit choice probabilities, without introducing an additional continuous (or discretized) state.

At the start of period $t$, after observing $(m_t,n_t)$, the household draws i.i.d.\ Extreme Value Type I shocks $(\zeta_t^{\text{keep}},\zeta_t^{\text{adj}})$ and chooses the branch that maximizes the shock-augmented conditional value,
\begin{align}
	v_t(m_t,n_t)
	&= \max\Big\{
		v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t) + \sigma_\zeta\,\zeta_t^{\text{keep}},\;
		v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t) + \sigma_\zeta\,\zeta_t^{\text{adj}}
	\Big\}, \label{eq:taste_discrete_EV}
\end{align}
where $\sigma_\zeta > 0$ is the taste-shock scale and $\ell_t = m_t+(1-\tau)n_t$. The conditional value functions $v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t)$ and $v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t)$ retain exactly the same Bellman definitions as in the baseline model: the taste shocks do not enter the inner continuous maximizations, so the first-order conditions~\eqref{eq:FOC_c_basic}--\eqref{eq:FOC_d_basic} and the continuous policies $c_t^{\text{keep}}$, $c_t^{\text{adj}}$, $d_t^{\text{adj}}$ are unchanged.

Because $(\zeta_t^{\text{keep}},\zeta_t^{\text{adj}})$ are i.i.d.\ Extreme Value Type I, the expectation of~\eqref{eq:taste_discrete_EV} has the closed-form log-sum representation
\begin{align}
	v_t(m_t,n_t)
	&= \sigma_\zeta \log\Big(
		\exp\big(v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t)/\sigma_\zeta\big)
		+ \exp\big(v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t)/\sigma_\zeta\big)
	\Big), \label{eq:taste_logsum}
\end{align}
with logit choice probabilities
\begin{align}
	P_t^{\text{adj}}(m_t,n_t)
	&= \frac{\exp\big(v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t)/\sigma_\zeta\big)}
	{\exp\big(v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t)/\sigma_\zeta\big)
	+ \exp\big(v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t)/\sigma_\zeta\big)},
	\qquad
	P_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t) = 1 - P_t^{\text{adj}}(m_t,n_t). \label{eq:taste_logit}
\end{align}
The expected (ex-ante) policy functions are the probability-weighted averages of the two branches,
\begin{align}
	c_t(m_t,n_t) &= P_t^{\text{keep}}\, c_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t) + P_t^{\text{adj}}\, c_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t), \label{eq:taste_c_combined}\\
	d_t(m_t,n_t) &= P_t^{\text{keep}}\, n_t + P_t^{\text{adj}}\, d_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t). \label{eq:taste_d_combined}
\end{align}
As $\sigma_\zeta \to 0$, the logit probabilities collapse to the hard indicators of the baseline model and~\eqref{eq:taste_logsum} collapses to the hard maximum. Larger $\sigma_\zeta$ spreads probability mass onto the inferior branch near indifference, so $c_t$ and $d_t$ (and the marginal-value functions derived from the inclusive value~\eqref{eq:taste_logsum}) are smooth in $(m_t,n_t)$. No quadrature over taste nodes is required: the EV Type I assumption delivers the inclusive value and choice probabilities in closed form. The taste shocks do not alter the household's budget set, so the no-borrowing constraint $a_t \ge 0$ is unaffected. I derive the envelope conditions, the Euler equations, and the EGM algorithm with EV Type I taste shocks in appendix~\ref{subsec:appdx_taste_basic}.

The inclusive value~\eqref{eq:taste_logsum} is differentiable in $(m_t,n_t)$ for any $\sigma_\zeta > 0$, so the terminal keep--adjust boundary is no longer a line. Figure~\ref{fig:tasteshock_T} plots the adjustment probability for $\sigma_\zeta \in \{0.1, 0.15, 0.2\}$. Where the two conditional values are close, the logit probability lies strictly between zero and one, and a larger scale widens that band because the household needs a larger value gap before it is willing to put almost all of its probability on the better branch. The combined rules~\eqref{eq:taste_c_combined}--\eqref{eq:taste_d_combined} are the same probabilities applied to the two branches. Around the old threshold the plotted consumption and durable policies are therefore mixtures of the keeper and the adjuster, rather than a jump from one to the other.

The jump at the old threshold and the spike in the marginal propensity are both replaced by a gradual transition between the keeper and the adjuster. A small taste scale is enough for that transition to be continuous. The same scale also moves probability onto the inferior branch at states far from indifference, which is the feature the steady-state comparison below will use to separate taste shocks from search.

\subsection{Extension: Search and stochastic prices}
\label{subsec:ModelBasicSearch}
\label{subsec:ModelBasicPrice}
\label{subsec:ModelBasicFull}

This subsection replaces the hard keep-versus-adjust maximum with directed search and a stochastic purchase price, introduced together. Extreme Value Type~I taste shocks and directed search are alternative smoothers of the discrete margin; nesting the two would double-smooth that choice, so the environment here uses search together with price risk on the adjuster's continuous problem. The household chooses a search intensity $\varepsilon_t\in[0,1]$ and, when it adjusts, buys the new durable at a realized relative price $p_t$.

Search effort is costly,
\begin{align}
	\sigma(\varepsilon_t) = S\big[\varepsilon_t + (1-\varepsilon_t)\log(1-\varepsilon_t)\big], \label{eq:search_cost_basic}
\end{align}
where $S>0$ governs the friction in the market for durables, following \cite{Khan2019-ew}. The purchase price is i.i.d.\ across periods and log-normal with mean one,
\begin{align}
	\log p_t &\sim \mathcal{N}\Big(-\frac{\sigma_p^2}{2},\, \sigma_p^2\Big), \qquad \mathbb{E}[p_t] = 1, \label{eq:price_dist}\\
	p_t &\perp \theta_t. \label{eq:full_p_dist}
\end{align}

Only a purchase of new durables is exposed to $p_t$. The household liquidates the existing stock at the fixed recovery rate $1-\tau$, so the liquidity available upon adjustment, $\ell_t = m_t + (1-\tau)n_t$, does not depend on the price. The keeper never buys, so $v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t)$ keeps its baseline Bellman definition. The adjuster's budget is
\begin{align}
	a_t = \ell_t - c_t - p_t\, d_t \ge 0, \label{eq:price_budget}
\end{align}
and the price-contingent value is
\begin{align}
	v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t,p_t) &= \max_{c_t,d_t} \ u(c_t,d_t) + \beta \mathbb{E}_t \big[ \bar v_{t+1}(m_{t+1},n_{t+1}) \big],\\
	& \text{s.t. equation~\eqref{eq:price_budget},}\\
	& \qquad m_{t+1} = \mathcal{R} a_t + \theta_{t+1}, \qquad n_{t+1} = (1-\delta)\,d_t.
\end{align}
The interest rate $\mathcal{R}$, the depreciation rate $\delta$, and the no-borrowing constraint $a_t\ge 0$ are unchanged. Next period's durable stock is measured in physical units, so $p_t$ changes the current cost of acquiring $d_t$, and the future service flow changes only through depreciation.

The adjuster's Lagrangian, with $p_t$ held fixed, has first-order conditions
\begin{align}
	\lambda_t^{\mathrm{adj}} &= u_c(c_t,d_t), \label{eq:price_foc_c}\\
	\lambda_t^{\mathrm{adj}} &= \beta \mathcal{R}\, \mathbb{E}_t\big[\bar v_{t+1}^m\big], \label{eq:price_foc_a}\\
	u_d(c_t,d_t) &= p_t\,\lambda_t^{\mathrm{adj}} - \beta(1-\delta)\,\mathbb{E}_t\big[\bar v_{t+1}^n\big]. \label{eq:price_foc_d}
\end{align}
In KKT form these are
\begin{align}
	u_c(c_t, d_t) &\geq \beta \mathcal{R}\, \mathbb{E}_t \big[\bar v_{t+1}^m\big], \\
	u_d(c_t, d_t) &\geq p_t\,\beta \mathcal{R}\, \mathbb{E}_t\big[\bar v_{t+1}^m\big] - \beta (1 - \delta)\, \mathbb{E}_t\big[\bar v_{t+1}^n\big],
\end{align}
with equality when the borrowing constraint does not bind. Because $p_t$ multiplies only $d_t$ in the budget, the nondurable condition is the baseline condition~\eqref{eq:FOC_c_basic} given $d_t$. The durable condition prices the marginal unit at $p_t$ rather than at one. The adjuster's continuous policies $c_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t,p_t)$ and $d_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t,p_t)$ are therefore solved at each price node, and the reported ex-ante rules are price-weighted averages of those state-contingent choices.

After $\theta_t$ has materialized and $(m_t,n_t)$ are known, the household chooses search intensity from the distribution of $p_t$, before the realization. A successful search then draws $p_t$. The household adjusts when the realized price is worth taking and otherwise keeps the current stock. The search cost is sunk, so a bad draw can be rejected. Let
\begin{align}
	v_t^{\mathrm{match}}(m_t,n_t)
	&\equiv \mathbb{E}_{p_t}\Big[\max\big\{ v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t,p_t),\; v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t)\big\}\Big]
\end{align}
be the expected value of a match. The discrete problem is
\begin{align}
	v_t(m_t,n_t)
	&= \max_{\varepsilon_t\in[0,1]}
	\Big[(1-\varepsilon_t)\, v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t)
	+ \varepsilon_t\, v_t^{\mathrm{match}}(m_t,n_t)
	- \sigma(\varepsilon_t)\Big]. \label{eq:full_discrete}
\end{align}
Write $\Delta(p_t) = v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t,p_t) - v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t)$ and $\bar\Delta(m_t,n_t) = \mathbb{E}_{p_t}[\max\{\Delta(p_t),0\}]$. Optimal effort is one number at $(m_t,n_t)$,
\begin{align}
	\varepsilon_t^\ast(m_t,n_t)
	= \max\left\{0,\;
	1 - \exp\big(-\bar\Delta(m_t,n_t)/S\big)
	\right\}. \label{eq:full_eps}
\end{align}
It does not depend on the realized price. Let $\mathbb{1}(p_t)=1$ when $\Delta(p_t)\ge 0$, and let $\pi_t=\mathbb{E}_{p_t}[\mathbb{1}(p_t)]$ be the probability that a draw is acceptable. The probability of buying is $\varepsilon_t^\ast\pi_t$. The ex-ante value that enters the previous period is
\begin{align}
	\bar v_t(m_t,n_t)
	&= (1-\varepsilon_t^\ast)\, v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t)
	+ \varepsilon_t^\ast\, v_t^{\mathrm{match}}(m_t,n_t)
	- \sigma(\varepsilon_t^\ast), \label{eq:full_barv}
\end{align}
which replaces $v_t(m_t,n_t)$ inside the keeper and adjuster problems one period earlier. The two mechanisms are complementary. Price dispersion raises $v_t^{\mathrm{match}}$ above $v_t^{\text{keep}}$ in states where adjusting at the mean price is worse but some draws are better, and that option value raises effort. Search governs whether a draw arrives. The realized price is the content of the draw.

This is the information order of Section~\ref{sec:RetirementModel}. There, effort is chosen from the distribution of the account factor, the realization is observed only if the option arrives, and the household then claims or denies the draw. The remaining difference is the date of the benefit. Retirement effort chosen at the end of period $t$ is the probability that an option arrives at $t+1$, so $\beta$ enters that effort rule and effort is irrelevant at the terminal date. Here the match and the acceptance decision fall in the same period, the effort rule has no discount factor, and search is defined in the terminal period because the benefit is the current comparison. The probability of retiring in that section is last period's effort times the probability that the hard maximum selects claiming. The probability of adjusting here is this period's effort times $\pi_t$.

Two limits isolate each mechanism. As $\sigma_p\to 0$, the price collapses to one. Then $\pi_t\in\{0,1\}$, and whenever adjusting is better, \eqref{eq:full_discrete} is the search problem
\begin{align}
	v_t(m_t,n_t) = \max_{\varepsilon_t \in [0,1]} \Big[(1-\varepsilon_t)\, v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t) + \varepsilon_t\, v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t) - \sigma(\varepsilon_t)\Big], \label{eq:search_discrete_basic}
\end{align}
with the same continuation one period ahead. As $S\to 0$, search is costless. Whenever $\bar\Delta>0$, effort goes to one, the household draws a price, and the acceptance decision is the hard maximum
\begin{align}
	v_t(m_t,n_t,p_t) = \max\Big\{ v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t),\; v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t,p_t)\Big\}, \label{eq:price_discrete}
\end{align}
whose expectation is
\begin{align}
	\bar v_t(m_t,n_t)
	= \mathbb{E}_{p_t}\Big[\max\big\{ v_t^{\text{keep}}(m_t,n_t),\; v_t^{\text{adj}}(\ell_t,p_t) \big\}\Big]. \label{eq:price_barv}
\end{align}
As $S\to\infty$, effort goes to zero and the household never adjusts. Both limits together, $\sigma_p\to 0$ and $S\to 0$, recover the hard-maximum baseline of Section~\ref{sec:ModelBasic}. The optimal effort rule, the envelope conditions, and the EGM steps are derived in Appendices~\ref{subsec:appdx_search_basic}, \ref{subsec:appdx_price_basic}, and~\ref{subsec:appdx_full_basic}.

\paragraph{Search alone.}
Shutting the price down ($\sigma_p = 0$) isolates the extensive margin. Effort is then the probability of moving from the keeper to the adjuster, and the closed form~\eqref{eq:full_eps} sets that probability to zero wherever adjusting does not raise value and raises it continuously once the gap is positive. The sharp boundary of the hard maximum therefore becomes a band of partial adjustment. Figure~\ref{fig:search_ss_T} plots that band in the terminal period for $S\in\{0.15,0.2,0.25\}$: a higher search cost makes the same value gap buy less effort, so the region of interior adjustment shrinks toward the states where adjusting is most valuable. One period earlier, the combined consumption and durable rules are the effort-weighted average of the two branches. Figure~\ref{fig:search_periodt_MPC_decomp} shows the consequence. The jump at the old threshold is replaced by a gradual transition, and the spikes in the marginal propensities, which in the baseline are the discrete switch from one branch to the other, are damped with it.

\subsection{Comparing smoothness mechanisms: Lifecycle}\label{subsec:ModelBasicSmoothingCompare}

The three extensions above each replace the hard keep-versus-adjust maximum with a probability that can lie between zero and one. This subsection asks whether that replacement produces comparable smoothness over a finite horizon, and whether one period's decision rule is enough to tell the devices apart. The answer to the second question is what sends the argument to the stationary distribution in Section~\ref{subsec:ModelBasicInf}.

Figures~\ref{fig:tasteshock_ss_T}, \ref{fig:search_ss_T}, and~\ref{fig:pricestd_Ss_T} are the same terminal adjustment region once each device is turned on. In the baseline of Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_ss_T} the household adjusts with probability one outside the $(S,s)$ band and keeps with probability one inside it. Under Extreme Value shocks that indicator becomes the logit probability~\eqref{eq:taste_logit}. It rises continuously through the old boundary, and a larger $\sigma_\zeta$ spreads the rise over a wider set of states, because a larger scale makes the household willing to take the inferior branch at a larger value gap. Under search with the price shut off, the indicator becomes effort. Effort is zero where keeping is better and rises with the value gap where adjusting is better, so the band is the set of states in which a match is worth paying for. Under price dispersion with search shut off, each realized price still has its own threshold, and the figure plots the probability that a draw from the price distribution falls on the acceptable side of that threshold. A larger $\sigma_p$ widens the set of states in which some draws are accepted and others are rejected. All three figures therefore replace that sharp boundary with a region in which the adjustment probability lies strictly between zero and one. The three regions are not the same object, which the kink counts make precise.

Table~\ref{tab:smoothing_kinks} counts the candidates that the upper envelope still has to delete, in the same earlier period as the policy figures below, on the raw endogenous-grid cloud before it is interpolated. For the keeper, a decreasing segment is a fold in the locus that maps end-of-period assets back to beginning-of-period market resources. Such a fold appears when next period's value is nonconcave, so the first-order condition is necessary and not sufficient, and the upper envelope of \citet{Iskhakov2017-jb} discards it. For the adjuster, the scan of the durable first-order condition returns several candidates at nearby levels of liquidity, and FUES keeps only those on the upper envelope of the value cloud. Under the hard maximum the keeper still has five decreasing segments, about $4.5$ percent of the locus, and the adjuster still loses four candidates. A taste scale of $\sigma_\zeta = 0.05$ and a search cost of $S = 0.1$ drive both counts to zero. The continuation that enters today's endogenous grid is already smooth enough that every first-order candidate lies on the envelope. Price dispersion at $\sigma_p = 0.1$ does the same for the keeper and leaves the adjuster with seventeen candidates still removed, about $3.5$ percent of the cloud summed over price nodes. Each price node is its own hard-maximum problem, so nonconvexity survives inside the price-contingent choice even after the ex-ante probability has been averaged.

Search alone has a further limitation that a deletion count cannot see. Where the expected gain from a match is not strictly positive, effort in~\eqref{eq:full_eps} is exactly zero, and the household keeps with probability one. Extreme Value shocks randomize that side of the threshold as well, because the logit assigns positive probability to adjustment at every finite value gap. Price dispersion alone randomizes through the draw and retains a threshold at each realized price. The specification whose smoothness is comparable to the taste shock is the pair: effort governs whether a draw arrives, and the household can still reject it, so the probability of buying is interior on both sides of the old boundary. A zero deletion count is also a statement about the raw cloud, not about the interpolant that is stored and plotted. Section~\ref{subsec:ModelBasicEuler} pairs these counts with off-grid Euler residuals.

The decision rule in a single period does not carry the distinction. Figures~\ref{fig:smoothing_shifts_levels} and~\ref{fig:smoothing_shifts_mpcs} hold the beginning-of-period durable stock fixed and overlay the combined consumption and durable rules, and their slopes with respect to market resources, as one scale is strengthened. Panels (a)--(c) are taste, search, and price dispersion, each with the other two shut off. In every one of those panels the combined nondurable rule shifts up, the combined durable rule shifts down, and the spikes in the marginal propensities around the band are damped. Near indifference the household puts weight on both branches. The adjuster liquidates the current stock, pays the proportional cost, and reoptimizes, which on these grids means more nondurable consumption and a smaller chosen durable than the keeper, who is stuck with $d_t = n_t$. Strengthening the smoother widens the set of states that mix the two branches, so the combined rule moves toward the adjuster's bundle. Panel (d) raises the standard deviation of the transitory income shock. Income risk changes the continuation value of both branches. It does not replace the discrete choice, and it is drawn next to the other three so that a smoother operating through the income process is visible and is kept out of the comparison.

This common period-$t$ shift is why the finite horizon cannot identify the three devices. In the retirement illustration, taste and search moved the worker's consumption function in opposite directions, so the decision rule itself separated them. Keep-versus-adjust reallocates a stock the household already owns, and it leaves the labor-income process unchanged, so the combined rules move together. The devices separate once households are allowed to accumulate. The probability of adjusting then changes how often the stock is reset and how much liquid wealth is held against that reset, which is a property of the stationary distribution rather than of one period's policy.

\subsection{Comparing smoothness mechanisms: Infinite horizon}
\label{subsec:ModelBasicInf}

The finite-horizon comparison showed that taste shocks, search, and price dispersion each replace the keep--adjust line with a band, and that one period's combined rules move in the same direction when any of the three is strengthened. Households in that exercise do not accumulate. The probability of adjusting changes how often the durable is reset and how much liquid wealth is held against the reset, so the three devices can separate only in a stationary distribution. This subsection solves each economy to a time-invariant policy and reads the comparison off the distribution of households on that policy.

Table~\ref{tab:inf_ss} reports the stationary means. $C$ is nondurable consumption, $D$ is the durable stock, $\mathrm{DEX}$ is expenditure on durables, $\mathrm{TEX} = C + \mathrm{DEX}$ is total expenditure, $A$ is liquid assets, and $\mathrm{ADJ}$ is the fraction of households that buy in a period, in percent. The first block is the Extreme Value economy by itself. Search is shut off, the purchase price is degenerate at one, and the taste scale runs through $\sigma_\zeta \in \{0.1,\, 0.15,\, 0.2\}$. The other two blocks are one search-and-price economy, solved twice as a comparative static, rather than two further experiments with the other mechanism shut off. Effort is chosen from the price distribution before the draw, as in~\eqref{eq:full_discrete}, and a bad draw is rejected. The second block holds the search cost at $S = 0.2$ and raises price dispersion through $\sigma_p \in \{0.08,\, 0.1,\, 0.12\}$. The third holds price dispersion at $\sigma_p = 0.1$ and raises the search cost through $S \in \{0.15,\, 0.2,\, 0.25\}$. The middle specification is the same household in both blocks, which is why that row appears twice. Search with the price shut off would be the one-sided limit of the previous subsection: effort is zero wherever keeping is better, and a higher search cost then need not reduce how often households adjust. The sign of $S$ is therefore read inside the pair, where the option value of a rejectable draw is what effort is buying.

\paragraph{Steady state.}

A larger taste scale raises the adjustment probability because the logit puts more weight on the inferior branch at every state, including states in which adjusting is only slightly worse than keeping. Households replace the durable for reasons that do not track the value gap. They carry a smaller stock, and they hold fewer liquid assets against a discrete expenditure that now arrives more often. From $\sigma_\zeta = 0.1$ to $0.15$ to $0.2$, the adjustment probability rises from $12.2\%$ to $18.6\%$ to $24.7\%$, the durable stock falls from $3.56$ to $3.27$ to $3.03$, and liquid assets fall from $0.93$ to $0.71$ to $0.60$. Nondurable consumption barely moves, from $0.80$ to $0.79$. The entire comparative static is a change in how often an exogenous shock selects the adjuster.

Price dispersion, inside the pair, raises the adjustment probability for a different reason and moves the stock the other way. At $S = 0.2$, raising $\sigma_p$ from $0.08$ to $0.12$ raises the adjustment probability from $7.7\%$ to $9.9\%$, and liquid assets fall from $1.39$ to $1.27$, while the durable stock rises from $4.28$ to $4.87$. The price distribution is normalized to mean one before the household accepts or rejects the draw, so a wider distribution is a wider spread of offers rather than a cheaper posted price. Unusually good draws are accepted and unusually bad draws are rejected, and the purchases that go through install a larger stock. Taste and price dispersion can therefore produce the same rise in the adjustment frequency while moving durables in opposite directions. An adjustment frequency by itself cannot tell those two mechanisms apart.

The search cost moves the adjustment probability in the remaining direction. At $\sigma_p = 0.1$, raising $S$ from $0.15$ to $0.25$ lowers the adjustment probability from $9.3\%$ to $8.3\%$. The durable stock falls from $4.59$ to $4.45$, and liquid assets rise from $1.27$ to $1.37$. Effort has become more expensive, so fewer matches arrive, replacement is less frequent, and the resources that would have been spent on those purchases stay in the liquid asset. The movements are smaller than in the taste block because effort responds where the option value of a draw is positive, whereas the logit randomizes every state. Across the two blocks of the pair, the parameters push the adjustment probability in opposite directions: wider price dispersion raises it, and a higher search cost lowers it. A target for that frequency therefore leaves $S$ and $\sigma_p$ separately free, which is the sense in which the pair can be disciplined by more than one moment. The quantitative step, matching the response of consumption and the response of the adjustment probability together, belongs to the fuller model and is not carried out here.

\paragraph{Labor Jacobians.}

The same stationary policies yield sequence-space Jacobians. A column of the Jacobian is the path of an aggregate after a one-time unanticipated change in an aggregate shock, so the date of the shock itself is the impact response and the later dates are the propagation. Figures~\ref{fig:Taste_Inf_Jacobians_labor}--\ref{fig:Combined_varS_InP_Jacobians_labor} plot that object for a rise in mean labor income, for the three blocks of Table~\ref{tab:inf_ss}. The paths describe dynamics around the steady states just reported. They are a different question from the signs in the table. On this grid a larger taste scale raises the impact response of nondurable consumption slightly, from $0.089$ at $\sigma_\zeta = 0.1$ to $0.095$ at $\sigma_\zeta = 0.2$, so the comparative static in the taste scale is not a dampening of the impact propensity to consume. Holding the search cost at $S = 0.2$ and varying price dispersion leaves that impact response in a narrow range, between $0.076$ and $0.079$. Holding price dispersion at $\sigma_p = 0.1$ and varying the search cost moves it: the impact response is about zero at $S = 0.15$, and it is $0.077$ and $0.060$ at $S = 0.2$ and $S = 0.25$. Any later exercise that matches impulse responses has to confront both the impact and the shape of these paths, including the search-cost case in which the impact response changes with $S$.

\paragraph{Kinks and Euler residuals}
\label{subsec:ModelBasicEuler}

Table~\ref{tab:smoothing_kinks} records cleanliness of the raw EGM cloud. The object that is stored and plotted is an interpolant of the refined cloud, so a zero deletion count does not by itself establish that the computed rule solves the problem that was posed. A policy mixed over dominated Euler roots can look as smooth as one whose envelope has nothing left to delete. I therefore pair the kink counts with off-grid Euler residuals in the sense of \citet{Judd1992-sp}. For an unconstrained continuous choice $c$ with implied Euler consumption $c^{\mathrm{impl}}$,
\begin{align}
	\mathrm{EE}(c)
	\;=\;
	\log_{10}\!\left(
		\left\lvert \frac{c - c^{\mathrm{impl}}}{c} \right\rvert
		+ 10^{-16}
	\right).
	\label{eq:judd_ee}
\end{align}
More negative is more accurate: $-4$ is a $0.01\%$ consumption error, $-3$ a $0.1\%$ error. Residuals are branch-conditional---keepers on $c_t^{\mathrm{keep}}$, adjusters on the durable first-order condition converted to the same consumption units ($\mathrm{EE}_d^{\mathrm{adj}}$)---and are never evaluated at the mixed policy $\bar c_t$, which does not satisfy the Euler equation even when both branches do. The test mesh is off the solution grid; constrained points are dropped. I report the mean and the 95th percentile, because the mean can look acceptable while a thin set of points near the S,s band is not.

Table~\ref{tab:smoothing_euler} places both diagnostics in the same period as Figures~\ref{fig:smoothing_shifts_levels}--\ref{fig:smoothing_shifts_mpcs}, now for every listed magnitude of $\sigma_\zeta$, $S$, and $\sigma_p$. For the keeper the computational claim holds. Decreasing segments are zero at all three taste and search values, and $\mathrm{EE}_c^{\mathrm{keep}}$ improves with the smoother: the hard-max mean and 95th percentile are $-4.19$ and $-2.50$; at $\sigma_\zeta=0.2$ they are $-4.52$ and $-3.39$, and at $S=0.3$ they are $-4.42$ and $-3.24$. The smoother combined consumption rule is not an average over dominated keeper roots. Price dispersion likewise zeros the keeper count at all three $\sigma_p$, with only a small deterioration in the mean (from $-4.11$ at $\sigma_p=0.05$ to $-4.00$ at $0.2$).

For the adjuster the deletion count is not enough. Taste and search eliminate FUES removals at every listed magnitude, but the 95th percentile of $\mathrm{EE}_d^{\mathrm{adj}}$ remains positive---$0.45$ to $0.69$ as $\sigma_\zeta$ rises, $0.27$ to $0.22$ as $S$ rises---while the mean stays around $-3$. Raising the smoother does not repair the tail. Price dispersion continues to remove points ($20$, $17$, $34$ as $\sigma_p$ increases) and has a similar tail. Cleanliness of the raw FOC cloud is not the same as accuracy of the stored $(c,d)$ interpolant, so FUES is not shown to be redundant by the deletion count alone. Taste, search, and prices are different economies and are not ranked by residual size; the residuals certify internal accuracy of each specification, not proximity to the frictionless target. Table~\ref{tab:smoothing_euler_inf} reports the same kink and Euler diagnostics for the infinite-horizon stationary policies of Section~\ref{subsec:ModelBasicInf}.
